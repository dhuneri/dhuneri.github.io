# Kalo Alaichi in the Ferment: A Two-Week Pickling Trial

- Published: 2026-11-14T17:30:00.000Z
- Updated: 2026-11-14T17:30:00.000Z
- Description: A refrigerated vinegar-pickle comparison with mustard seed and black cardamom—not a documented fermentation.
- Author: Risita Sedain
- Tags: Recipes

## First, name the jar correctly

Vinegar-pickled radish and fermented radish may both taste sour, but they do not become sour in the same way. A quick pickle uses added acid. A fermentation depends on microbial activity under controlled conditions. The two are not interchangeable merely because both are packed into a jar.

The requested two-week fermented trial has not been carried out. Rather than invent observations or give an unvalidated fermentation procedure, this article offers a refrigerator-pickle comparison based on University of Maine Cooperative Extension’s method. It asks a narrower question: how does adding black cardamom seed change a mustard-seed radish pickle over refrigerated storage? It cannot establish how fermentation changes aroma. [\[1\]](#ref-1)

## Four jars, two conditions, two dates

Prepare four clean pint-size heatproof jars with lids: control/day 2, cardamom/day 2, control/day 13, cardamom/day 13\. This keeps the day-13 samples closed until their tasting and avoids repeatedly introducing utensils into the same jars. Ingredients: 4 cups cleaned, thinly sliced winter radish total, divided equally; 2 cups vinegar labeled 5% acidity; 2 cups water; 2 tablespoons pickling/canning salt; 4 teaspoons granulated sugar; 4 teaspoons dried mustard seed, divided equally; 0.40 g black cardamom seeds total, divided into two 0.20 g portions for the two treatment jars.

The four-jar quantities double Maine’s two-jar sour brine recipe. Each jar gets the same amount of radish and mustard seed; only the two treatment jars receive cardamom. The addition of a small amount of dried cardamom is an untested adaptation of the source’s permitted dried-spice category, not a separately validated commercial process. Mustard is a common allergen; identify it clearly for tasters. [\[1\]](#ref-1)

## Method | Follow the acid and the cold

1. Wash hands and work surfaces. Rinse radish under running water, peel if needed, and slice evenly. Pack approximately 1 cup into each clean heatproof pint jar; place 1 teaspoon mustard seed in each. Add 0.20 g lightly cracked black-cardamom seed to each of the two treatment jars, using a scale that can resolve the dose.
2. In a saucepan combine the full 2 cups 5% vinegar, 2 cups water, 2 tablespoons pickling salt and 4 teaspoons sugar. Stir, bring to a boil and boil for 2 minutes as in the Maine method. Carefully pour hot brine over the vegetables, covering them and leaving approximately ½ inch headspace. Use heatproof jars and avoid thermal shock. Apply clean lids and refrigerate promptly at 4°C or below. Do not reduce vinegar, add extra water or substitute vinegar of unknown acidity. [\[1, 2\]](#ref-1)
3. Let flavor develop for at least 1–2 days. Open only the two day-2 jars on day 2 and the two day-13 jars on day 13\. Use clean utensils every time; keep jars refrigerated. This is a refrigerator pickle, not a shelf-stable preserve: do not can it or store it at room temperature. Use within the source’s two-week window; day 13 is deliberately inside it. Discard if the cold chain is interrupted or contamination is suspected. Smell and appearance alone do not prove safety.

## The planned tasting

Serve equal pieces of radish, not just spoonfuls of brine, in randomly coded samples. At each tasting compare control and cardamom from the same day; use the same serving temperature and provide water. Record sourness, mustard intensity, cardamom/resinous impression, bitterness, texture and preference on separate 0–5 scales. Do not tell tasters in advance that acid ‘should’ suppress or intensify the spice. Record the actual words used. [\[3\]](#ref-3)

The planned design does not isolate acidity as a variable: every jar uses the same vinegar brine. It tests the perceived effect of adding cardamom at two storage times. To study acidity itself, a food-safety specialist would need to design and validate suitable acid levels; do not alter the vinegar-water ratio in this household protocol.

## What would be needed for the fermentation story

A genuine fermentation feature needs a validated radish-specific method, appropriate salt and temperature controls, food-safety oversight, documented pH measurements, and an actual day-by-day record. A measured pH alone is not proof of safety, and ‘bubbles’ are not a certificate. The role of cardamom in such a process remains an open research question, not a result to invent.

For the jar on this page, report the result honestly: a refrigerated vinegar pickle with a proposed cardamom comparison. If tasters find no meaningful difference on day 2 or day 13, that is a finding worth keeping—provided someone actually makes and records the trial.
