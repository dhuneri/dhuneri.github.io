# Moisture vs. Water Activity: Why a 10% Dry Pod Can Still Spoil in Storage

- Description: Understanding available water, mold growth, and relative humidity inside packaging barriers.

## The number on the sack is not the whole story

A buyer who records “10% moisture” has recorded something useful. They have not, by that fact alone, proved that every capsule in the sack will remain sound through a wet storage season. The number describes the amount of water in a sampled product at a particular time. It does not describe how readily that water is available to microorganisms, how evenly it is distributed or how much humidity the package will admit later.

For Nepal’s large cardamom, *Amomum subulatum*, this distinction matters precisely because drying is not the end of the process. Capsules may cool unevenly, wait in a humid room, travel in sacks, or sit against a wall that becomes cold at night. These are possible routes to trouble, not observations from a particular Nepalese farm. No measurements were supplied for this article.

## Two measurements, two different questions

Moisture content, on a wet basis, is the mass of water divided by the total mass of the sample, multiplied by 100\. A 100 kg lot at 10% wet-basis moisture contains, in a simplified accounting, 10 kg of water and 90 kg of dry solids. A dry-basis figure uses dry solids as the denominator and will be numerically different. Always report the basis and the method: an inappropriate oven method can also drive off volatile material, not just water.

Water activity, *a*w, is a ratio of vapor pressures at the same temperature. At equilibrium, *a*w equals the equilibrium relative humidity in a closed headspace divided by 100\. Thus an equilibrated 60% headspace corresponds to *a*w 0.60\. A single reading of the room humidity is not the same thing: a room and a sealed bag can be out of equilibrium, and humidity inside a package can vary with temperature and location. [\[1, 2\]](#ref-1)

Think of moisture content as an inventory and water activity as a condition of availability. The binding behavior depends on the material, temperature and its moisture history. Two foods—or two differently processed lots of the same spice—need not have the same water activity at the same moisture percentage. Only a measured, relevant sorption relationship can connect the two with confidence.

## What the cardamom literature actually says

An Indian storage study of flue-cured large cardamom reported that material with 12.9% initial moisture equilibrated to 67% relative humidity at 27°C; the abstract also reported storage stability at 11% moisture and 60% relative humidity. Under an accelerated 38°C and 90% RH condition, the authors reported heavy fungal growth and substantial volatile-oil loss within 45 days. Those are study-specific observations, not a forecast for a Nepalese lot, every package or every storage period. The available source is the abstract; details needed to evaluate replication and full methods were not accessible. [\[3\]](#ref-3)

Equilibrium at 67% RH corresponds to *a*w approximately 0.67 only under the definition and temperature conditions of that equilibrium measurement. It does not tell us the water activity of an unrelated lot at 10% moisture. Nor does it establish that all fungi grow at 0.67 or that everything below a single number is safe: species, temperature, time and local wetting matter. Low water activity restricts growth; it does not necessarily destroy spores or toxins already present. [\[1, 2\]](#ref-1)

| Wet-basis moisture    | 10.0%             | 12.0%                     |

This is arithmetic, not a prediction that a bag will reach 12% or that 12% is unsafe. An actual prediction requires a product-specific sorption isotherm, package water-vapor transmission, ambient temperature and humidity, package geometry and storage duration. Local wet spots can be hazardous even if the lot average looks acceptable.

## A measurement plan that survives contact with a storehouse

Start at intake. Identify the lot, drying method and date; record package type, location and temperature. Take a representative composite across the lot and preserve individual subsamples if uneven drying is suspected. Measure moisture by an appropriate validated spice method and water activity with a calibrated instrument after the sample and chamber reach a controlled, documented temperature. Record method, calibration checks, sample preparation, replicate results and uncertainty. A hand-held room hygrometer is a useful warning instrument, not a substitute for a calibrated product *a*w measurement. [\[1\]](#ref-1)

At packing, allow hot product to cool in a clean, dry protected space before sealing. Sealing warm material into a cooler environment can create a condensation risk if local surfaces fall below the relevant dew point; the outcome depends on actual temperatures and humidity, not on a universal waiting time. Inspect seals and keep packages away from wet floors and walls. Protect a clean lot from rain and damaged packaging throughout transport.

During storage, follow a documented sampling schedule appropriate to the risk and test suspicious areas separately. A little spreadsheet can carry lot ID, date, position, temperature, room RH, package condition, moisture, *a*w and any visible defects. If mold is found, isolate the affected material and seek qualified food-safety assessment; do not assume that scraping, heating or re-drying reverses contamination. Agree acceptance limits and corrective actions with the buyer and the applicable standard before the season begins.

## The disciplined conclusion

The disciplined conclusion is less dramatic than “10% is unsafe,” but more useful: 10% describes a sample at one moment. Safe storage requires a controlled product and a controlled package over time. Measure both the water that is there and the conditions that let it move.
