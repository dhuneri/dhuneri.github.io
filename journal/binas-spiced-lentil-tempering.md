## The ingredients and the logic

*jimbu* is a dried Himalayan allium used in Nepalese cooking, including as a tempering for dal. One Nepalese culinary source describes frying it in ghee or mustard oil before adding it to a dish. That background supports the technique in general; it does not establish Bina’s personal practice, Suketar-specific custom or this particular recipe. [\[1\]](#ref-1)

Black cardamom is a separate, assertive ingredient. Its pod can be lightly cracked to expose the seeds, infused briefly in oil and removed before serving. The dried *jimbu* can scorch quickly, so a cook needs to attend to the pan rather than use the clock alone. The recipe is a starting proposal, not a tested result.

## Proposed recipe | Four portions

Ingredients: 200 g split red lentils (masoor dal), rinsed; 900 ml drinking water, plus extra if needed; ½ teaspoon ground turmeric; 5 g fine salt, adjusted to taste; 15 ml food-grade culinary mustard oil, or a neutral cooking oil if preferred; 0.50 g whole black cardamom pod, lightly cracked; 0.30 g dried *jimbu*; 1 small dried red chili, about 0.5–1 g, optional. Use a fine scale for the spice if available.

Equipment: a saucepan, small metal tempering pan, lid, spoon and fine strainer or a way to retrieve the pod. Mustard can cause allergic reactions. Use only mustard oil legally sold and labeled for culinary use in your jurisdiction; never use essential oil or a product labeled for external use. If *jimbu* is unavailable, omit it; chives are not equivalent, and fresh herbs behave differently in hot oil.

## Method | Dal first, tempering last

1. Put rinsed lentils, water and turmeric in a saucepan. Bring to a simmer, skim foam if needed and cook uncovered or partly covered for about 20–30 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the lentils are soft. Add more hot water if the dal becomes too thick. Stir in the salt and keep gently hot while preparing the tempering.
2. Put the 15 ml oil in a small pan over medium-low heat. Add the cracked cardamom and dried chili. Warm gently for about 30–45 seconds, moving the pan; do not let the oil smoke or leave the pan unattended. Take the pan off the heat, add the dried *jimbu* and swirl briefly until fragrant. The exact time depends on pan and heat; stop before the herb blackens or smells burnt. If anything burns, discard the tempering and make a fresh one.
3. Carefully pour the warm tempering into the dal, keeping your face and hands clear of splatter; a lid can shield the pan. Stir, rest two minutes, and remove the pod and chili before serving if desired. Serve with plain rice and a vegetable side. For a gentler version, reduce the cardamom in a new preparation, not by guessing how much has already infused.

## How to test the balance

To evaluate the proposed recipe, reserve a small portion of plain cooked dal before tempering. Compare it with the finished dal at the same temperature, using small samples and clean spoons. Record whether *jimbu*, chili and cardamom can each be perceived and whether the dal still tastes like lentils. A second run can test 0.25 g versus 0.50 g pod while keeping the dal and all other ingredients constant. These are hypotheses, not observations made in Bina’s kitchen.

The comparison is not a blinded proof of individual ingredient effects: the tempering changes several variables at once. To isolate the pod, make otherwise identical temperings with and without cardamom and randomize the tasting order. Record the oil brand, pod lot, quantities, timing and any scorching.
